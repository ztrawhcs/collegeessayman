The trap DC students walk into

Growing up here means growing up around politics, policy, and adults with impressive job titles. So a very large number of essays from DC students are about an internship on the Hill, a march, a campaign, a model UN gavel, or the summer they learned that Change Is Slow.

Those essays are rarely bad. They are just indistinguishable. An admissions officer who reads the DC region has seen every version of the earnest policy awakening, and none of them tell her what the applicant is actually like at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday.

The interesting essay from a DC kid is usually the one they dismissed as too small — the Metro ride, the job at the ice cream place, the argument with a sibling that they still have not won. My whole method is getting that one out of them, which takes a conversation, not an editor.

DC has no state flagship, and that changes the whole essay workload

A student in Virginia or Maryland has an obvious in-state option and a short list. A DC student does not, so the list runs wider and further from home — and every additional school on it is another supplement to write.

DCTAG makes that concrete. The District's Tuition Assistance Grant pays the gap between in-state and out-of-state tuition at participating public universities, up to $10,000 a year, for students who have been domiciled in DC for at least twelve months. It is the reason a DC family's list often includes a dozen state flagships across the country, and it is the reason the supplements — not the personal statement — are usually where the real hours go.

Georgetown is its own case. It began accepting the Common Application in the fall 2026 cycle as part of a three-year pilot, alongside its own longstanding application, with neither route favored. What did not change is that Georgetown requires its own distinctive supplemental essays either way, and they are not the questions the rest of your list is asking.
