The transfer essay does a job the freshman essay never has to

A high school senior writes about who they are. A transfer applicant has to do that and answer two questions that contradict each other in the wrong hands: why are you leaving, and why here?

Answer the first one honestly and you risk sounding like someone who blames institutions for their own unhappiness. Answer it carefully and you sound evasive, which reads worse. The essays that work do something harder — they treat the current school fairly, sometimes generously, and still make the move sound inevitable.

I have written that paragraph about myself. It is genuinely difficult, and almost nobody gets it right on the first pass.

"Why here" has to be checkable

Freshman applicants get some latitude to be aspirational. Transfer applicants do not, because you have already been at a university and you are supposed to know what you need from one. A reader who has your file is asking whether you researched this place or whether you are running.

So the destination half of the essay is a research problem before it is a writing problem: a named course, a specific faculty member's work, a program structure that does not exist where you are. Vague admiration is the single most common way a transfer essay fails.

You are usually applying with less time and more evidence

The good news is that you have a college transcript, a college recommender, and a year or more of adult life to draw on, which makes for a far less strained essay than most seventeen-year-olds can write. The bad news is that transfer deadlines arrive while you are in the middle of a term at the school you are trying to leave.
