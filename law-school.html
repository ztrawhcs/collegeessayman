Why this one is different from every other application essay

In college admissions the personal statement sits inside a thick file — supplements, activities, recommendations, a counselor letter. In law admissions it is usually alone. Two pages, double spaced, and at most schools nothing else qualitative. Your numbers do the sorting; the statement is the only place a human learns anything about you that the spreadsheet did not already say.

That raises the stakes on a decision most applicants make badly: what the essay is about. The default is a narrated résumé, or the moment you decided to become a lawyer. Committees read thousands of both. They have read the mock trial one, the immigration story one, the injustice-witnessed-at-sixteen one.

What they have not read is yours, told the way you would actually tell it.

The addendum is not the personal statement, and mixing them costs you

A soft semester, a low score, a gap, a disciplinary note — those belong in an addendum, stated plainly and briefly, without apology or narrative. Applicants who instead build the personal statement around explaining a weakness spend their only two pages on defense.

We separate them. The addendum handles the fact. The statement does the work only it can do.
