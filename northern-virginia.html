The problem that is specific to here

Virginia's public universities draw an enormous share of their classes from these few counties. That is good news for admission odds and terrible news for essays, because it means the reader at Charlottesville or Williamsburg is working through a very large stack of applications from students who went to similar schools, took similar courses, did similar activities, and — this is the part nobody warns you about — wrote about them in similar ways.

When I read a draft from a student at Langley or Madison or TJ, the writing is usually competent. Competent is the problem. An admissions officer reading four hundred essays from Fairfax County is not looking for another well-organized paragraph about a service trip, a robotics team, or the season a shoulder injury ended. They are looking for the one applicant who sounds like a person.

So the first session is almost never about writing. It is about finding the story that only your student could tell, which is usually not the one they arrived with, and frequently not the one they thought was impressive.

The in-state supplement nobody takes seriously enough

Students spend months on the main personal statement and then write the "why this school" supplements in a weekend. That is backwards for Virginia applicants, because the supplements are where the reader finds out whether you actually know the place or whether you pasted its name into a paragraph that would work for any university with a lawn.

A supplement that could be sent to three schools with the name swapped is not an essay. It is a form. We fix those by finding the specific, checkable thing — a course, a professor's research, a building you have actually stood in — and building outward from it.
